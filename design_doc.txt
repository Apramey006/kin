For Kin as you’ve designed it, someone who was recently diagnosed / in the early stage is the much better target user.
In early-stage dementia, people can often still have meaningful conversations, recognize many people and places, use technology, and live fairly independently—but may need cues for names, people, appointments, or familiar places. That lines up almost perfectly with Kin’s “small contextual whisper” idea. (Alzheimer’s Association)
For example:
They see someone and know they’re familiar but can’t retrieve the name.
Kin: “That’s Daniel, your nephew. You saw him at Maya’s birthday last month.”
The goal is not to replace their memory, but to give just enough information for them to reconnect the dots themselves.
Late-stage dementia is a much weaker fit for the wearable assistant. In severe Alzheimer’s, people may have major difficulty communicating, recognizing family, understanding context, or interacting with a device, and often require around-the-clock care. At that point, nonverbal interaction—familiar voices, music, photos, touch, sounds, and presence—can become more important. (NIA)
Interestingly, though, Kin could still have a late-stage use case—but for caregivers rather than as an AI whispering facts to the patient. The family's collection of voices, photos, and stories could help caregivers choose familiar music, recordings, photographs, or reminiscence experiences.
So for the hackathon I would define your user very precisely:
“Kin is designed for people recently diagnosed with early-stage dementia who are still independent, but are beginning to lose small pieces of everyday context.”
That actually makes the project stronger because the problem becomes very specific: help someone stay independent for longer by giving the smallest possible memory cue at the moment they need it.


It starts with the family, not the person with dementia. A relative opens Kin and adds a few memories: photos of important people, short voice notes, names, relationships, and stories like “This is Sarah, your daughter. You two always get coffee on Sundays.” Kin organizes those into a private family memory bank. Then the person with early-stage dementia uses Kin on their phone with earbuds. When they need help, they can point the camera at a person, photo, or object, or tap a “Help me remember” button. Kin searches the family memories. If it finds a strong match, it quietly says something like: “That’s Sarah, your daughter. You usually have coffee together on Sundays.” If Kin isn’t confident, it says nothing instead of guessing. The other side is for the family. Kin notices missing pieces in the memory bank and asks relatives questions such as: “You mentioned Grandpa’s cabin, but nobody has recorded where it was. Do you remember?” Their answer becomes a new memory Kin can use later. So the basic flow is: Family records memories → Kin organizes them → wearer asks for context → Kin gives a short cue only when confident → Kin asks the family to fill missing memories. For the hackathon demo, you only need to prove this with one family, maybe 3 relatives, 10–20 memories, a phone camera, and earbuds.

## The scoped concept

**Kin — a family-built memory layer.**

Each family member gets a small **Keeper Agent** trained only on the memories *they contributed*: photos, short voice recordings, names, relationships, and stories.

The wearer has a lightweight phone app + earbuds. When something in their environment matches a stored memory strongly enough, the Keepers compete to offer context.

But the important technical twist is:

> **Kin would rather say nothing than invent a memory.**

A separate **Gatekeeper** checks whether the evidence is strong enough before anything reaches the wearer.

Meanwhile, a **Family Weaver** looks across everyone's memories and asks relatives for the *missing pieces* that would make the family's collective memory richer.

That gives you two impressive AI loops:

```text
WORLD → MEMORY RETRIEVAL → MULTI-AGENT CONSENSUS → CONFIDENCE GATE → WHISPER
                                      ↑
                                      │
FAMILY ← STORY REQUEST ← MEMORY GRAPH GAPS ← FAMILY WEAVER
```

## What I would actually build in 24 hours

Do **three surfaces**, not a giant product.

| Surface        | What judges see                                                                                  |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Kin Family** | Relatives upload photos + record 30-second stories                                               |
| **Kin Wearer** | Phone camera / microphone identifies relevant context and whispers a cue through earbuds         |
| **Kin Weaver** | A living family-memory graph that finds a missing connection and asks the best person to fill it |

Everything else is backend magic.

Suppose I'm the granddaughter.

I upload three photos and record:

> “This is Aunt Nora. Grandma and Nora used to bake lemon cake every Sunday. Nora always wore that ridiculous yellow apron.”

Kin extracts:

```text

EVENT
Sunday lemon-cake baking

OBJECTS
yellow apron
lemon cake

PLACE
Grandma's old kitchen

SOURCE
Maya, granddaughter

EVIDENCE
photo_14
voice_note_3
```

Store the raw media, transcript, embeddings, and extracted graph.

The impressive part is that **Maya's Keeper only has access to Maya's memories**.

Mom's Keeper has Mom's memories.

Uncle David's Keeper has David's memories.

So instead of pretending there is one omniscient AI, you literally model the fact that families remember things differently.

## 2. The swarm moment

This should be the centerpiece of the demo.

Someone points Kin at a photo/object/person.

Maybe the demo camera sees:

**Nora wearing the yellow apron.**

The orchestrator sends the observation to all Keepers simultaneously.

```text
MAYA KEEPER
"I think that's Nora.
Evidence: photo_14
Confidence: .94"

DAVID KEEPER
"Looks like Aunt Nora.
Evidence: photo_22
Confidence: .89"

MOM KEEPER
"No reliable memory."
```

Then your Gatekeeper computes something like:

```text
visual similarity      0.93
memory retrieval       0.91
keeper agreement       0.95
source provenance      1.00
contradiction penalty  0.00

FINAL = 0.93
```

Threshold = `0.85`.

Only then does Kin whisper:

> “That's Nora. You two used to bake lemon cake together on Sundays.”

If confidence is `0.62`:

**nothing happens.**

That silence is a feature.

Put that confidence pipeline visibly on-screen during the judge demo. Watching three agents retrieve evidence, disagree, and then seeing the system decide **SPEAK / STAY SILENT** will feel substantially more sophisticated than another RAG chatbot.

For a hackathon prototype, I would make contextual recognition **user-triggered** — tap the earbud/app or say “Kin?” — rather than promise an always-listening medical wearable.

That's an excellent demo payoff.

## Don't actually build “agents trained on relatives”

That phrase sounds impressive but will destroy your schedule.

Fake the expensive interpretation; implement the useful interpretation.

A Keeper is essentially:

```text
Keeper {
    relative_id
    vector_namespace
    permitted_media[]
    relationship_graph
    retrieval_prompt
}
```

When context arrives:

```python
results = parallel(
    maya_keeper.retrieve(context),
    david_keeper.retrieve(context),
    mom_keeper.retrieve(context)
)
```

Each Keeper returns **structured data**, not prose:

```json
{
  "claim": "person is Nora",
  "memory_ids": ["photo_14", "story_3"],
  "confidence": 0.94
}
```

Then one synthesis model turns grounded claims into a gentle sentence.

You'll get the visual effect of a swarm without building a fragile autonomous-agent architecture.

## The confidence system is your technical differentiator

Don't let the LLM decide `"confidence": 0.94` because it feels like 0.94.

Calculate it from signals.

Something like:

$$
C =
0.35V +
0.25R +
0.20A +
0.15S -
0.25X
$$

where `V` = visual/audio match strength, `R` = retrieval similarity, `A` = agreement between independent Keepers, `S` = source support, and `X` = contradiction score.

Then:

```text
C ≥ .85          whisper normally
.70 ≤ C < .85    optional cautious cue
C < .70          SILENT
contradiction    SILENT
no provenance    SILENT
```

For the hackathon, I'd actually simplify further:

**Only implement whisper and silence.**

No ambiguous middle state.

That gives you a fantastic demo:

```text
Known family photo   → ✓ WHISPER
Similar stranger     → ○ SILENCE
Known object         → ✓ WHISPER
Random object        → ○ SILENCE
```

The judge immediately understands why your architecture matters.

## Scope ruthlessly

Your actual feature checklist should be only:

1. Upload photo.
2. Record story.
3. Convert both into searchable memories + graph nodes.
4. Create a separate memory namespace per relative.
5. Take a camera snapshot as context.
6. Run Keepers concurrently.
7. Run deterministic confidence gate.
8. Speak a grounded cue.
9. Demonstrate silence on insufficient evidence.
10. Identify one graph gap.
11. Ask one relative a highly specific question.
12. Record answer and visually update graph.

Anything beyond those twelve things is secondary.

No medication reminders. No wandering detection. No emergency monitoring. No diagnosis. No calendar. No actual continuous surveillance. No attempt to claim improvement of dementia symptoms.

Position it as a **family memory and reminiscence-support prototype**, not a medical decision system.

## And I would slightly change the pitch

Your existing concept is good, but the differentiator gets buried. I'd pitch it more like:

> **Kin gives a family a collective memory.**
>
> Every relative gets a Keeper: an AI grounded only in the photos, voices, and stories they've contributed. When someone with memory loss needs context, the Keepers quietly compare what they remember and offer a cue **only when the evidence agrees. When they aren't sure, Kin stays silent.**
>
> Behind the scenes, a Family Weaver maps those memories together, discovers the stories the family hasn't recorded yet, and asks the person most likely to remember.
>
> **The family doesn't train an AI. The family remembers together.**

That last loop — **human memories → swarm → safe cue → missing-memory detection → human contributes new memory → swarm gets better** — is the part I'd make feel insane.

The winning technical story isn't “we put GPT in earbuds.” It's **a provenance-aware, uncertainty-gated, multi-agent collective memory that actively repairs its own knowledge graph by asking humans.**
